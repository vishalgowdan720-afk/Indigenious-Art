About Indigenous Art of India

❦
🎨

Living Traditions

Indigenous art forms of India are not relics — they are living languages. Passed through generations by word of hand, these traditions encode cosmology, ritual, ecology, and identity within every stroke and stitch.

🌿

Natural Materials

Practitioners use mud, rice paste, natural dyes, plant fibres, and handspun threads — materials sourced from the earth that connect the artwork to its landscape and community.

🏛️

GI Protected Heritage

Several indigenous art forms carry Geographical Indication (GI) tags, legally protecting their authenticity and ensuring that artisans receive recognition and economic rights for their ancestral knowledge.

The Three Traditions

Indigenous Art Forms

❦
Maharashtra

Warli Art

Ancient tribal paintings using geometric forms — circles, triangles, and squares — to narrate scenes of daily life, harvest, and ritual on mud-plastered walls.

GI Tagged Tribal Wall Art
Bihar

Madhubani Art

Vivid paintings from the Mithila region depicting mythology, nature, and social events with bold outlines, intricate fillings, and no empty space left unpainted.

GI Tagged Mithila Mythology
Karnataka

Kasuti Embroidery

A traditional needlework from northern Karnataka, featuring intricate geometric patterns inspired by temples, palanquins, and lotus motifs, worked entirely without knots.

GI Tagged Embroidery Textile

Academic Foundation

Literature Survey

❦

Research and academic sources that informed this project.

01

"Adaptation and Development of Designs Inspired from Warli Art"

📖 Source: International Journal of Applied Research, 2017

This paper examines how Warli's geometric vocabulary — circles, triangles, and dots — is being adapted into contemporary textile and product design while maintaining its tribal identity. The study explores the tension between commercialisation and cultural preservation.

Key Insight: Geometric minimalism is Warli's core language. Adaptation must preserve community ownership.
02

"The Study of Indian Art and Culture – Madhubani"

📖 Source: Shodhganga Repository, Lalit Kala Akademi Archives

A comprehensive cultural study of Madhubani painting traditions in the Mithila region of Bihar, documenting the role of women artists, the shift from mud walls to paper/canvas, and the development of sub-styles such as Bharni, Kachni, Tantrik, Godna, and Kohbar.

Key Insight: Madhubani is matrilineal — passed from mother to daughter — and each sub-style carries distinct ritual functions.
03

"Kasuti Embroidery: History, Raw Materials and Tools"

📖 Source: Textile Design Journal, Dharwad University, Karnataka

This study documents the four primary Kasuti stitching techniques — Gavanti, Murgi, Negi, and Menthi — along with raw materials (silk thread, cotton fabric) and the socio-cultural context in which Kasuti garments were produced for wedding trousseaux in northern Karnataka.

Key Insight: Kasuti is made without knots — a feat of skill that demands both artistic precision and cultural knowledge.
